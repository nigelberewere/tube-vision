---

*[Screen: a savings account statement. Balance: $12,000. Interest earned this year: $48.]*

That's a real savings account statement.

Twelve thousand dollars. Twelve months. Forty-eight dollars in interest.

The bank calls that a savings account.

*[Beat.]*

In the same twelve months, inflation ran at roughly 3.4%.

Which means that $12,000 needed to grow by $408 just to keep its value.

It grew by $48.

*[Pause.]*

You didn't save $12,000 this year.

You lost $360.

And the bank sent you a statement that made it look like a win.

## CONTEXT — 0:40 to 2:00

---

*[Simple graphic: purchasing power of $10,000 over 10 years at 0.4% interest vs 3% inflation.]*

Here's the thing nobody explains when you open a savings account.

The number going up is not the same as your money growing.

There's a difference between your balance and your purchasing power.

Your balance is the number in the app.

Your purchasing power is what that number can actually buy.

And when inflation runs faster than your interest rate — which it has done for most of the last fifteen years — those two numbers move in opposite directions.

*[Beat.]*

Your balance goes up.

Your purchasing power goes down.

The bank profits from the spread between what they pay you — 0.4% — and what they lend your money out at — 6, 7, 8 percent.

You are not a saver.

You are a source of cheap capital.

*[Pause.]*

So what do you do instead? That's what this video is actually about.

## ACT 1 — The three traps that keep people in savings accounts
### 2:00 – 5:00

---

*[Clean numbered graphic: Trap 1 / Trap 2 / Trap 3 — reveal one at a time.]*

There are three reasons smart people stay in savings accounts longer than they should.

**Trap 1: The safety illusion.**

Savings accounts feel safe because the number only goes up.

But safety is not "the number doesn't drop." Safety is "my money doesn't lose value."

By that definition, a savings account isn't safe. It's just slow.

*[Beat.]*

**Trap 2: The effort gap.**

Moving money into something better — a high-yield account, index funds, Treasury bills — requires a decision, an account, a transfer.

The savings account requires nothing. It just sits there.

And humans are wired to do nothing when doing something feels uncertain.

The banks know this. They built their business model on it.

*[Beat.]*

**Trap 3: The wrong emergency fund math.**

You've been told to keep three to six months of expenses liquid for emergencies.

That's correct.

But "liquid" does not mean "in a 0.4% savings account."

High-yield savings accounts are equally liquid — same FDIC protection, same withdrawal speed — and pay ten to fifteen times more interest.

*[Pause.]*

Most people don't know this because the bank that holds their checking account also offers them a savings account — and makes it very easy to say yes to that one.

---

## ACT 2 — What to actually do
### 5:00 – 8:00

---

*[Simple three-tier graphic: Emergency fund / Medium-term / Long-term.]*

Here's the framework. Three buckets. Simple.

**Bucket 1: Emergency fund — High-yield savings account.**

Three to six months of expenses. Nothing less. Keep it here.

High-yield savings accounts currently pay 4.5 to 5.2% annually — more than ten times the national average savings rate.

Same FDIC protection. Same liquidity. More money.

There is no argument for keeping your emergency fund in a standard savings account in 2024.

*[Beat.]*

**Bucket 2: Money you'll need in 1–5 years — Treasury bills or CDs.**

If you're saving for a house, a car, a wedding, or anything you'll need within five years — the stock market is too volatile.

But savings accounts are too slow.

The middle ground: Treasury bills and certificates of deposit currently pay 4.8 to 5.4% with essentially zero risk.

You lock the money away for three, six, or twelve months. You get a guaranteed return. No drama.

*[Beat.]*

**Bucket 3: Everything else — index funds.**

Money you won't need for more than five years belongs in the market.

Not individual stocks. Not crypto. Not your brother-in-law's business idea.

A broad index fund — tracking the S&P 500 or the total market — has returned an average of around 10% annually over the last century.

Inflation runs at around 3%.

The gap between those two numbers is the difference between building wealth and treading water.

*[Pause.]*

Three buckets. That's it.

The complexity in personal finance is almost always manufactured.
